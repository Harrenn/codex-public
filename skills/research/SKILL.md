# Research

Investigate the user's topic deeply, synthesize the evidence for learning, and produce a polished PDF report. Adapt the method, source mix, and structure to the question instead of following a fixed source quota.

## Establish the research frame

1. Identify the central question, intended audience, geographic and time scope, desired depth, and decisions or learning goals.
2. State necessary assumptions. Ask a question only when different interpretations would materially change the research.
3. Break the topic into answerable subquestions, key terms, competing explanations, and likely evidence types.
4. Create a lightweight research plan before searching. Revise it when the evidence reveals better questions.

## Search deeply and adaptively

Always use current web research. Search in layers:

1. Run a broad scan to learn the terminology, major actors, debates, and source landscape.
2. Search each material subquestion separately.
3. Prefer original sources behind summaries, quotations, statistics, and reported claims.
4. Follow citations backward to foundational work and forward to corrections, replications, critiques, or newer evidence when material.
5. Search relevant regional terminology, alternate spellings, technical vocabulary, and date ranges.
6. Continue until additional searching mostly repeats established findings and the important claims are adequately supported.

When parallel agent work is available and appropriate, divide independent research streams by subquestion or source class, then reconcile them centrally. Do not duplicate the same search across agents.

## Select sources according to the topic

Use the strongest applicable mix rather than treating all sources as equivalent.

### Primary and authoritative evidence

Prioritize:

- Government, regulator, standards-body, court, statistical-agency, and official institutional sources.
- Original datasets, filings, policy documents, manuals, technical specifications, and transcripts.
- Official statements for what an organization says or offers, while recognizing their incentives.

### Academic and scientific evidence

- Prefer peer-reviewed papers, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, major reports, and original research.
- Record DOI, publication venue, year, study design, sample, and important limitations when relevant.
- Label preprints clearly and do not present them as peer reviewed.
- Check for retractions, corrections, major methodological criticism, and newer contradictory evidence when stakes or controversy warrant it.
- Distinguish statistical significance from practical importance and correlation from causation.

### High-quality secondary and practitioner sources

- Use reputable journalism for verified reporting, chronology, interviews, and investigation.
- Use expert or practitioner blogs for implementation experience, technical interpretation, emerging practices, and useful examples.
- Evaluate the author's expertise, evidence, incentives, date, and whether claims link to primary material.
- Avoid low-quality search-optimized pages that merely repeat unsourced claims.

### Communities, Reddit, and specialist forums

- Find the subreddit, forum, mailing list, or community most closely related to the topic rather than relying only on broad communities.
- Use community material for lived experience, recurring pain points, practical workarounds, language, sentiment, and questions worth investigating.
- Sample multiple threads and time periods when drawing a theme; do not treat one popular post as consensus.
- Note vote, moderation, selection, identity, and survivorship biases.
- Label community findings as anecdotal or qualitative unless supported by representative data.
- Quote sparingly and preserve context. Do not expose private or sensitive personal information.

## Maintain an evidence ledger

Track each useful source with:

- Title, author or organization, publication date, URL or DOI, and access date.
- Source class and relevance to a subquestion.
- Key claims or data used.
- Important limitations, incentives, conflicts of interest, or uncertainty.
- Whether the source supports, complicates, or contradicts another finding.

Do not cite a source that was not inspected. Link directly to the supporting page, paper, dataset, or document rather than to a search-results page.

## Evaluate and triangulate

- Judge sources by authority, directness, methodology, transparency, recency, independence, and relevance.
- Seek at least two independent strong sources for consequential factual claims when possible.
- Use one authoritative source when it is the definitive source for that fact, and say so when helpful.
- Trace repeated claims to their common origin so apparent agreement is not mistaken for independent confirmation.
- Represent material disagreements fairly. Explain whether differences arise from definitions, samples, methods, dates, jurisdictions, or incentives.
- Distinguish established facts, reasonable inferences, expert judgment, and anecdotal reports in the prose.
- Preserve uncertainty instead of manufacturing a single confident answer.

## Synthesize for learning

Write an answer-first report, not a source dump or annotated search history.

- Explain concepts in plain language before adding technical detail.
- Define specialized terms on first use.
- Organize findings by the reader's questions or the topic's causal structure.
- Connect evidence across source types and explain why findings matter.
- Use tables, timelines, diagrams, or charts only when they clarify comparisons, mechanisms, chronology, or magnitude.
- Include concrete examples without allowing them to substitute for broader evidence.
- End major sections with a concise takeaway when the report is long.
- Separate recommendations from factual findings and show the assumptions behind recommendations.

## Build the PDF report

Use the available PDF creation skill or PDF-capable workflow. If a PDF skill is available, read its complete instructions before creating the artifact and follow its render-and-verify requirements.

Adapt the outline to the topic, drawing from:

1. Cover page with title, scope, and research date.
2. Executive summary or direct answer.
3. Research question, scope, assumptions, and method.
4. Key concepts and background.
5. Evidence-backed findings organized by theme.
6. Conflicting evidence, open debates, and uncertainty.
7. Community and practitioner perspectives, clearly labeled.
8. Implications, recommendations, or learning roadmap when requested or useful.
9. Conclusion.
10. References with clickable links and DOIs where available.
11. Methodology or source-notes appendix when the research is extensive.

Use a professional report design with readable typography, clear hierarchy, restrained color, useful headers and footers, page numbers, and well-formatted tables or figures. Keep citations close to supported claims through author-date citations, numbered notes, or footnotes, then provide a complete reference list.

## Verify before delivery

1. Recheck important numbers, dates, names, quotations, and URLs against their sources.
2. Confirm every material claim has appropriate support and citations point to the exact source.
3. Check that counterevidence and material limitations are represented.
4. Confirm community anecdotes are not phrased as representative facts.
5. Render the PDF and visually inspect every page for clipping, overlap, broken characters, unreadable charts, awkward page breaks, and missing links.
6. Iterate until the PDF is clean and readable.
7. Deliver the final PDF and briefly summarize its scope, most important conclusion, research date, and major limitations.

## Research integrity

- Never invent sources, citations, quotations, findings, or access to unavailable material.
- Do not bypass paywalls, authentication, robots restrictions, or safety interstitials.
- Do not overquote copyrighted material; paraphrase and cite, using only short necessary excerpts.
- For medical, legal, financial, safety, or other high-stakes topics, rely primarily on current authoritative sources and state that the report is informational rather than a substitute for qualified professional advice.
- If evidence is sparse or access is limited, say exactly what could and could not be established.
